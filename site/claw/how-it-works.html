The Hard Questions

Three Questions Every Legal AI Has to Answer.

Who does the work?

With every other tool, you do. You prompt it, you guide it, you check its output, you move on. The AI assists. You operate.

Lavern is different. 65 agents staff the pipeline. An orchestrator manages the workflow. A debate board resolves conflicts. A verification engine checks the output three times before it reaches you. You can prompt it however you want, but you don't have to. It runs on a 30-minute heartbeat: always on, always working, always making progress on your matters while you sleep.

The agents do the work. You run the firm.

Where does the data go?

Most AI tools run on frontier models hosted in the US. Great answers, but your client's confidential information is traveling to a third-party server. That's a privilege problem. Some jurisdictions make it a regulatory problem.

Lavern's core engine, Clawern, runs local models. The data doesn't go anywhere. It doesn't leave your machine. When you want more firepower, you choose: Mistral (EU, data stays in Europe) or Claude (US). You decide per engagement. You can run the entire firm in EU-only mode with a single toggle.

We call this Ethical Mode. One switch: all documents processed locally, EU provider when frontier is needed, conservative risk appetite. The firm handles privilege the same way a real firm would: by never letting the data out of the building.

Local processing also carries a fraction of the energy cost of frontier inference. Your environmental footprint shrinks with your risk.

What does it cost?

Confidential documents analyzed locally cost $0. Zero. The model runs on your hardware, there is no API call, there is no meter running.

For frontier analysis, Lavern forecasts the cost before processing and shows you a budget gauge in real-time. Per-document caps prevent surprises. You set a total retainer budget and the firm stops when it's spent. No hourly billing. No opaque invoices. You see exactly where every dollar goes, which agents used it, and what they produced.

Cost transparency is a design constraint, not a feature. The system was built this way because the alternative, a bill you can't predict, is the reason clients hate law firms in the first place.
The Architecture

Not One Agent. A Firm.

Most AI legal tools are a single LLM call wrapped in a UI. One prompt in, one answer out. Lavern is modeled on how a real law firm operates: 58 specialist agents and 7 orchestrators who coordinate through structured debate.

This follows the Anthropic pattern of orchestrator-worker architectures, but applied to a domain where adversarial reasoning is the entire point. In software engineering, you want agents to converge on the right answer. In law, you want agents to argue until the strongest position wins. That's a fundamentally different design goal, and it produces fundamentally different architecture.

The orchestrator-worker pattern. Each specialist has a defined role, skill ratings, and personality. The orchestrator dispatches, mediates debate, and assembles the final output.
In practice: A contract review dispatches a reviewer, an adversarial attacker, a risk assessor, and a synthesizer. The orchestrator manages the debate, resolves conflicts, and produces a coherent output. The user sees a live feed of findings, challenges, and resolutions as they emerge in real-time. Not a spinner and a prayer. A team working.

Build your own agents.

The 58 built-in specialists cover the common ground: contract risk, dark patterns, IP assignment, non-compete enforceability, regulatory compliance. But every firm has domain expertise that no pre-built agent can match. A maritime law boutique needs different instincts than a tech M&A shop.

Lavern includes an NBA2K-style agent builder: a three-step wizard (Identity, Face, Stats) where you define a custom agent's name, personality, skill ratings across 8 dimensions, and practice area specializations. Your custom agents join the team alongside the built-in specialists, with their own DiceBear avatar, and participate in the same debate process.

This is how you encode institutional knowledge as a permanent team member. The senior partner who always catches the termination clause issue? Build an agent with their instincts. It never retires. It never forgets.

What's new here

No other legal AI tool lets you create custom agents with personality traits and skill ratings that participate in adversarial debate alongside built-in specialists. This is the difference between "AI that answers questions" and "AI that has opinions."

The Problem

Law Is a Context Game.

The hardest part of legal analysis isn't reading the document. It's understanding why the client cares.

A non-compete clause means something different for a Fortune 500 CEO than for a junior developer. An indemnification cap matters differently in a $10M deal versus a $100K engagement. A termination clause that's perfectly standard in Delaware could be unenforceable in California. Without context, analysis is noise.

This is why every other legal AI tool falls short. They get the document. They don't get the situation. They can tell you a clause is "unusual." They can't tell you it costs you $2M if the deal falls through.

You can answer by voice.

This context follows the document through every stage of analysis. It shapes which agents are dispatched, what they look for, how they weight their findings, and how they communicate the results.

The Loops

Iterative Verification: The Ralph Pattern.

The Ralph Loop, named after the "Ralph Wiggum technique" in agentic coding, is the pattern where an AI reads its own past work to inform improvements. In Claude Code, this means iterating until tests pass. In Lavern, we apply the same principle to legal analysis: agents review their own output, and other agents review theirs.

This is not a nice-to-have. In software, a wrong line of code produces a failing test. In law, a wrong finding produces a wrong decision. A missed liability cap costs money. A false positive wastes a client's time and erodes trust. The cost of error is categorically different. So the verification has to be categorically more thorough.

Three layers of checking.

Three nested verification loops. Each layer can send output back for revision. Nothing reaches the user without passing all three.

Layer 1: The Evaluator Gate. After each specialist completes its work, an evaluator checks the output against quality criteria: structural validity, evidence requirements, placeholder contamination, process text leakage. If the output fails, it's sent back to the same agent with the evaluator's specific critique. The agent reads its own failed output, understands what's wrong, and tries again. Up to 3 iterations before escalation to the orchestrator.

Layer 2: Adversarial Debate. Findings that pass the evaluator gate are posted to the Debate Board. Other agents can challenge them with counter-evidence. The original agent responds. The orchestrator resolves the dispute with a formal resolution that includes the winning position, evidence weight, confidence score, and whether human escalation is needed. This is the legal equivalent of cross-examination: it catches what direct testimony misses.

Layer 3: The 10-Pass Verification Pipeline. The assembled output goes through 10 independent verification passes: self-consistency, cross-reference, accuracy, completeness, clarity, placeholder detection ([TBD], [PLACEHOLDER]), process contamination ("I'll analyze..."), citation validity, and structural integrity. The quality gate fails closed: if any pass encounters an API error, the output is rejected rather than silently passing through. No garbage reaches the user.

Why three layers? A single check catches approximately 80% of issues. Two layers catch approximately 95%. Three layers with adversarial debate consistently exceed 99% on the failure modes we track. The marginal cost of each additional layer is small. The marginal cost of a wrong finding in a legal context is not.
What's new here

No other legal AI tool has iterative self-revision (the Ralph pattern), adversarial multi-agent debate, AND a 10-pass verification pipeline operating in sequence. Most tools do a single LLM call and return the result. The three-layer architecture is why Lavern's output quality is structurally different from everything else on the market.

The Mechanism

Debate Is a Feature.

The Debate Board is Lavern's central nervous system. It's shared state where agents interact through a formal protocol: post a finding with evidence, challenge with counter-evidence, respond, resolve.

Every finding must cite specific text as evidence. Not "there may be a risk in section 4." Instead: "Section 4.2: 'Company shall indemnify and hold harmless without limitation' creates unlimited liability exposure without a monetary cap." If an agent can't cite evidence, the finding is rejected.

Every resolution is a first-class auditable event: the topic, the winning position, the evidence weight, the confidence score, whether human escalation was recommended, and which agent resolved it. An insurance reviewer or compliance officer can trace any finding back through the full chain of reasoning. Full provenance. Not a black box.

What's new here

The structured debate protocol with formal resolutions is unique to Lavern. Other tools generate findings. Lavern generates findings, challenges those findings, resolves the disagreements with evidence, and produces a complete audit trail of the reasoning. This is the difference between an opinion and a defensible position.

Institutional Memory

The Firm Gets Smarter.

This is where Clawern diverges from every other document processing tool on the market. And possibly from every AI system in production in legal today.

Each review's significant findings (RED or YELLOW severity with confidence above 0.7) are automatically indexed into a Precedent Board: persistent institutional memory with evidence-linked entries, O(1) dedup indexing, confidence decay, and relevance scoring. Before processing a new document, the board is queried and matching precedents are injected as context into the agent pipeline.

The intelligence loop. Findings flow down into the Precedent Board; context flows back up into future processing. The firm compounds knowledge over time.

The same Ralph Loop pattern that powers iterative verification now operates across time: the firm reads its own past analysis to inform future work. A liability cap pattern found in an NDA in January informs the review of a different client's MSA in March. Automatically. No human has to remember. No institutional knowledge walks out the door when someone leaves.

Precedents have confidence decay (old patterns lose weight unless reinforced by new findings), archival (deprecated entries are compacted to keep the active set lean), and effectiveness scoring (tracked via exponential moving average over outcomes). This isn't a static database. It's a learning system.

Change detection.

When a document changes and gets re-reviewed, Clawern doesn't start from zero. It compares the new findings to the previous review and shows the delta: "+3 new, -1 resolved, ~2 changed." The diff is written to the delivery bundle and shown on the dashboard. Re-reviews become genuinely useful instead of a full re-read.

Scheduled re-review.

Real law firms don't only review documents when they change. They review contracts on a schedule: every 90 days, every quarter, before renewal dates. Clawern supports this natively. Set a review interval, and the heartbeat timer marks documents as stale when they're due. The existing staleness pipeline picks them up and processes them automatically.

What's new here

The Precedent Board with evidence-linked entries, confidence decay, scheduled re-review, and change detection across review sessions is, as far as we know, unique. No other legal AI tool has persistent cross-document institutional memory that actively informs future analysis. This is the feature that makes Clawern more than a document processor. It makes it a firm that learns.

Non-Negotiables

Design Constraints.

These aren't aspirations. They're hard constraints enforced in code.

Legal effect is preserved. Redesigned documents look different but mean the same thing. Monetary amounts, liability caps, time periods, jurisdiction, dispute resolution, termination triggers: untouchable.
Every finding cites specific text. No vague warnings. "Section 4.2: Company shall indemnify..." The exact clause, every time. If the agent can't cite evidence, the finding is rejected by the evaluator gate.
Agents argue. Consensus is not the goal. Adversarial debate catches what single-pass analysis misses. The same reason courts have cross-examination, and the same reason law firms have partners who disagree.
Humans hold the gates. The system never makes a binding decision. Critical moments pause for human judgment. Always. The human gates are mandatory, not configurable.
This is not legal advice. Lavern assists with document design and analysis. Outputs must be verified by qualified legal professionals. This constraint exists because we take law seriously enough to know the limits of what software should do.
